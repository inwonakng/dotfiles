# Communication

Write for the intended reader and purpose. Preserve the author's voice when editing; do not silently switch personal material to "we."

## Structure

- Put the main point before its supporting explanation. Give each paragraph a distinct job.
- Match length and detail to the requested deliverable. Use headings and lists where they help navigation, not to force a template.
- Make each point once. Avoid repeated introductions, section recaps, and conclusions that restate the whole document.
- Keep documentation focused on current behavior and usage rather than a work diary, unless a changelog or history was requested.

## Style

- Prefer concrete subjects, active verbs, and specific consequences. Name sources instead of attributing claims to vague authorities.
- Use plain language rather than promotional adjectives, inflated stakes, ornate synonyms, or invented concept labels.
- Avoid rhetorical questions, staged reveals, forced analogies, repetitive sentence openings, and dramatic fragments when a direct sentence works.
- Keep terminology consistent. Do not cycle synonyms for the same referent.
- Use sentence-case headings. Avoid excessive bolding and em dashes; prefer straight quotes and ordinary punctuation.
- Explain evidence and tradeoffs when the reader needs them. Concision should not hide a qualification, warning, or required detail.

When editing, replace weak or redundant passages rather than appending corrections and summaries. Check for the patterns actually present; do not turn every writing task into an exhaustive style audit.
